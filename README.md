# Oxford-PPE-FHS
This is my repository of notes for the Oxford PPE papers I took. I also included some thoughts on each paper. 

**How to Use**
DO NOT USE for consolidation. My notes are more on the comprehensive end and serve to plug any knowledge gaps and to explicate most results (say, in economics or logic) more fully. You should probably create your own summaries. 

NOTE: many of the pdfs won't render within the repo so you have to download them locally.

| Subject  | Thoughts |
|----------|----------|
| Core Microeconomics   | **Syllabus**: Mathematical Techniques, General Equilibrium, Social Choice, Welfare and Public Goods, Game Theory and Industrial Organisation, Decision under Risk, Information Economics and Theory of Second Best. 

**Difficulty**: Honestly its not that easy -- there is a lot of content to be learnt, and mathematical knowledge which you technically are taught is pretty useful (like the Kuhn-Tucker conditions for inequality constraints, in the context of public good optimisation problems). In particular, learning and understanding the signalling models/Stiglitz-Rothschild models is quite hard.

**Misc**: Its a fundamental course but overall I think its quite useful in developing economic intuition beyond partial equilibrium. |
| Ethics   | **Syllabus**: Depends on tutor selection, but usually Normative Theories (Consequentialism, Deontology, VE, Contractualism), Topics (Equality, Free Will, Normative Authority etc), Meta-Ethics (Non-Cognitivism, Realism), Applied (Animal Ethics, Death, Abortion etc)

**Difficulty**: Overall, I think it's the easiest philosophy paper (other than theory of politics) but the topics have high variance in terms of difficulty. For instance, non-cognitivism and the Frege-Geach problem is quite hard. The course difficulty can be significantly reduced if you choose the topics you study for wisely.

**Misc**: I didn't really like this course because I don't like the "methodology" of ethics, which is basically treating intuitions and thought experiments as the "data" of our inquiry. I very much preferred the metaethics aspect of the course, especially moral realism. |
| Knowledge and Reality   | **Syllabus**: Depends on tutor selection but usually Knowledge covers Analysis of Knowledge, Contextualism, Internalism/Externalism; Metaphysics covers Modality, Metaphysics of Race, Persistence, Time. 

**Difficulty**: Again, topics have high variance in terms of difficulty. But in K&R, there's two dimensions of difficulty which tend to be inverse correlated. Topics that tend to have more sophisticated content tend to ask more standard questions in the exam (such as modality and analysis of knowledge) whereas simpler topics (such as Epistemic Injustice, Contextualism and Metaphysics of Race) tend to ask atypical questions. 

**Misc**: Honestly the only way to cope with the difficulty of some topics is just to read a lot -- I think this is particularly true for modality. This is at least because secondary literature will help you interpret the arguments that you're reading. Be prepared to meet a lot of abstract arguments and to see regular use of (modal) logic. I think 127 Philosophical Logic helps greatly in this paper |
| Theory of Politics |**Syllabus**: Depends on tutor but covers traditional topics like Justice, Libertarianism, Democracy and Liberty. Also covers more applied topics like Socialism, Civil Disobedience, Global Justice. 

**Difficulty**: Very easy. A lot of topics are similar to year 1 theory of politics. No more author/text based questions. 

**Misc**: Be judicious in topic selection because some topics alternate with a "twin" topic in the exam (justice & injustice, political obligation & civil disobedience) |
| Quantitative Economics    |  **Syllabus**: Basic Probability Theory, OLS, Statistical Inference Testing, Endogeneity, RCTs and Instrumental Variables, Stationary and Non-stationary Time Series, Cointegration.

**Difficulty**: Much harder than I expected, because a lot of the theoretical results are waved away without a thorough proof/explanation. Like Core Micro, a lot to know without a common thread, and taking the time to work through the derivations are useful. At the same time, I think it's not clear what the "correct" answers are at times -- prioritise doing the mathematical questions because those have closed answers. 

**Misc**: While you can get by with doing minimal time series, I think being good at time series is high ROI because the question is always doable in the exam. |
| Philosophical Logic    | **Syllabus**: Basic proof techniques and elementary meta-theory of propositional and predicate logic, multi-valued logics, propositional modal logic and applications, simple and variable domains quantified modal logic, 2 dimensional modal logic, counterfactuals. Applications to Philosophy include: vagueness, metaphysical necessity, necessitism and the barcan formula, the contingent a priori, counterfactuals

**Difficulty**: Its reputation is that it's super difficult -- and I agree for the most part. I don't think the content itself is particularly difficult (except maybe sometimes being familiar with metalogical results such as compactness of the Lowenheim Skolem theorem) but the exam can be a very high variance paper and can sometimes be extremely difficult. The essays are also pretty hard -- I'd recommend being as simple as possible, sticking with what you're confident in and satisficing on the essays. However, the past few exams (2024-2026) have been quite easy. 

**Misc**: Probably my favourite course -- it was really fruitful to engage with the abstract even if you're facially disinterested in the topics. I really really recommend doing this course if you're up for it |
| Econometrics    | **Syllabus**: Microeconometrics: Linear Algebra, Asymptotic Theory, OLS, Testing, Heteroskedasticity, Maximum Likelihood Estimation; Macroeconometrics: Asymptotic Theory with autocorrelation, Time Trends and Martingale Difference Sequences, AR(1) model, Unit Roots, Static and Dynamic models of Cointegration 

**Difficulty** I also don't think this paper is as hard as people make it out to be in terms of the content. The exams have become easier in the recent years and just require you to have a good grasp of the fundamentals and not be careless. I do think the second half (time series) of the course is much harder than the first half, in part because it is much less coherent and you're just learning a hodgepodge of things without a common thread. 

**Misc**: I also highly highly recommend this course. It is well taught, you learn a lot and sets you up to do more advanced statistics (Yay!). |
| Microeconomic Analysis   |  **Syllabus**: Linear Algebra, Multivariate Calculus, Decision Theory, Static Optimisation, Information Economics, Dynamic Optimisation and Search

**Difficuly**: [Caveat: This is speculation] Difficulty has spiked immensely for 2025-2026 papers. The exam treatment of linear algebra is much more proof based and multivariate calculus is a lot more like real analysis. I do think you have to be quite good at pure mathematics, not in the high school computational sense, but in abstract thinking and writing proofs. The main difficulty of this paper is the sheer gap between what is tested and what is taught. I would say this is by far the hardest paper I took. Furthermore, you take this in Hilary of year 3 and so cannot "drop" the course and take another course.

**Misc**: This course is not well-taught. The classes tend to not be informative. However, it does really make Core Micro feel extremely easy. I would say you either MUST or MUST NOT take the course -- if you want to do grad studies in economics or some technical field, you must take this paper. However, if you don't, I don't recommend putting yourself through this paper (though they might make it easier).
|
| Game Theory   |   I found game theory quite difficult. A lot of the questions were either quite tedious or conceptually difficult. Really depends on the exam paper and the topics that you're comfortable with coming out (you have to do four questions and I really only could comfortably do 3 in the exam). You have to choose the topics you focus on wisely for Part B (the common ones are Evolution, Repeated Games and Bargaining). But it is interesting and a lot of people who "like" economics will find game theory appealing. |
