**TIPS FOR KNOWLEDGE AND REALITY**

In general, I suggest studying 5 topics (not everything comes out). Some topics are very broad and require much more studying than others (for example, modality is one such topic that has many things to study for versus something like epistemic injustice or contextualism) but may be more your cup of tea. I studied 5 topics: contextualism (didn't come out), modality, epistemic injustice, persistence, metaphysics of race. I eventually ditched Analysis of Knowledge because I found that too difficult; the easy topics tend to be those that are more self contained (epistemic injustice, metaphysics of race) and also those with very tractable debate structures (persistence, modality). Though to caveat, modality is conceptually challenging, but has a tractable debate structure (clearly delineated schools of thought).  


**NOTE: MODALITY**

I think that the content of modality is *particularly* difficult but also good to do for the exam -- this is because the views are pretty well delineated. The issue is that the topic is quite technical. I recommend reading supplementary content that is not always given. Here are some extra stuff to read:  

On Hyperintensionality/Against the (Kripkean) Modal Framework of Metaphysics (Simple Possible Worlds Semantics)
1. Kit Fine (1994) 'Essence and Modality'
2. Timothy Williamson, 'Essencnes, Heuristics and Metaphysical Illusions' (Reply to Fine: first half helps a lot with understanding the moral of Fine's paper)

On problem of Island Universes/Advanced Modalising Issues
1. Cian Dorr, 'How to be a Modal Realist' (First part before Counterparting)

Epistemological Challenges
1. Amie Thomasson (2020), 'Norms and Necessity' (Chapter 7 -- 'Epistemological Advantages' has an excellent exposition)
2. Peter Fritz, 'A Recombinatorial Puzzle'

Understanding Possible World Semantics/Metaphysical Necessity
1. Nathan Salmon (1989), 'The Logic of What Might Have Been' (Might be a bit challenging for those without prior modal logic background)
